The Meridian Course

Instructor's Learning Summary — Competencies & Learning Objectives

This document gives instructors, program directors, and CME/CE reviewers a complete map of the program's educational design: the competency framework, module-level learning objectives, behavioral assessment anchors, the pedagogy behind the six-step rhythm, and facilitation guidance for cohort use. The Meridian Course is a simulation-based program in which healthcare leaders "round" on strategic situations across a persistent fictional health system, pairing one game-theoretic lens per module with the TeamSTEPPS and communication tools that act on it.

Format: 8 modules · ~2–2.5 hours each · web-based, self-paced or cohort   |   Audience: clinical and operational healthcare leaders   |   Version: 1.0

Pedagogical design

The program is built on four evidence-based commitments:

1 · Deliberate practice over exposition

Each module's concepts are taught briefly (vignette + Socratic coaching), then immediately exercised in scored, branching simulations where AI counterparts hold their own incentives, histories, and hidden information. Counterparts resist weak moves and reward skilled ones; success requires performing the target behaviors, not describing them.

2 · Perspective-taking as a repeated mechanic

After every one-on-one simulation, the Switch Chairs activity replays the identical scenario with roles inverted: the learner plays their former counterpart. Empathy is not a module in this program; it is rehearsed eight times, each time revealing the counterpart's constraints, fears, and rational logic from the inside.

3 · Advocacy-inquiry debriefing (Debriefing with Good Judgment)

Every module closes with a structured debrief in the Rudolph et al. tradition: the debriefer pairs an observation and honest interpretation with a genuine question ("I noticed X; it made me think Y; what was going on for you?"). Debriefs always end in a written, time-bound commitment to practice applied to the learner's real organization — and Modules 4, 7, and 8 explicitly revisit earlier commitments, creating longitudinal evidence of transfer.

4 · A persistent world that makes the repeated game felt

Seventeen recurring personas carry stable incentives, communication styles, and memory across modules. Relational choices compound: trust built (or spent) with the charge nurse in Module 3 changes what is possible in Module 7's coalition. This architecture teaches the program's central lesson structurally — in healthcare, you never play a one-shot game.

Competency framework

Six competencies organize the program. Each module develops several; the capstone requires all six at once. (Mapping: ● = primary development site, ○ = reinforced.)

CompetencyDefinition
C1Strategic DiagnosisRead situations as games — identify players, strategies, payoffs, and information; distinguish cooperation, coordination, information, and mechanism failures, because their fixes differ completely.
C2Structured Team CommunicationDeploy TeamSTEPPS tools (SBAR, check-back, CUS, two-challenge rule, briefs/huddles/debriefs) as strategic instruments that create common knowledge and psychological safety — not as ritual.
C3Negotiation & InfluenceExcavate interests beneath positions, compute and improve BATNAs, defend against anchors, create value before claiming it, and build coalitions through pivotal-player analysis and honest tailoring.
C4Relational Capital & RepairPlay repeated games well: choose strategies that sustain cooperation, forgive noise without licensing patterns, deliver costly apologies, and visibly reward the first mover who speaks up.
C5Ethical Reasoning & LegitimacyApply utilitarian, deontological, and justice frameworks as complementary error-detectors; design procedurally just processes; deliver decisions people will hate with honesty, empathy, and transparency.
C6Systems & Mechanism DesignRedesign rules so ordinary self-interest produces desired outcomes: incentive compatibility, Goodhart's law, pre-mortems, counter-metrics, and humane transition design.

Module learning objectives & assessment anchors

Objectives are written to be observable in the module's own simulations; "the learner will be able to…" is implied throughout.

Round 1 · Executive suite · C1 C2

Morning Report — The Games We Are Already Playing

Prisoner's Dilemma · tragedy of the commons · psychological safety
Learning objectives
  • Identify the players, strategies, payoffs, and information structure in a workplace conflict, and name when a Prisoner's Dilemma or commons structure is operating.
  • Predict the equilibrium behavior a given incentive structure will produce, and explain why exhortation fails to change it.
  • Design an intervention that changes payoffs (protection, verification, reversion) rather than appealing to goodwill.
  • Demonstrate acknowledgment-before-advocacy when proposing cooperation to a previously exploited counterpart.
  • Surface a hidden group dynamic in a committee without attributing blame to any individual.
Simulations & scoring anchors

One-on-one: capacity-sharing proposal to a burned peer director — acknowledgment before advocacy; payoff-changing structure; trust trajectory.

Meeting: capacity committee — system framing; blame avoidance; psychological-safety moves that surface held-back information.

Debrief focus: where a Prisoner's Dilemma operates in the learner's organization, and one changeable payoff.

Round 2 · ED → inpatient medicine · C1 C2

The Handoff — Information Asymmetry and Signaling

Signaling & screening · cheap talk vs. costly signals · SBAR as protocol
Learning objectives
  • Distinguish cheap talk from costly signals, and weight messages by what they cost the sender.
  • Design screening questions that force different underlying realities to produce audibly different answers.
  • Conduct a structured information handoff using targeted screens and check-backs that convert disclosures into explicit plans.
  • Apply pressure for information without hostility, preserving the sending relationship for future exchanges.
  • Reframe SBAR and structured handoffs as mechanisms that solve information asymmetry — and redesign a message format so critical content cannot be compressed out.
Simulations & scoring anchors

One-on-one: the 55-second handoff from a rushed ED attending — screen design; closed loops; pressure without hostility.

Meeting: M&M-style handoff review — system framing over trial; blame defused; a forced-field signaling protocol produced and co-owned.

Debrief focus: the learner's own asymmetries — including where they are the compressing sender.

Round 3 · Community hospital · C2 C4

Repeat Customers — Reputation, Trust, and the Shadow of the Future

Repeated games · Axelrod's tournaments · repair after defection
Learning objectives
  • Classify relationships as one-shot or repeated games and select strategies (generous tit-for-tat) accordingly.
  • Apply the noise test — run one clarifying question before retaliating against an apparent defection.
  • Deliver a costly apology: name the specific act and audience, own the harm without an exculpatory clause, and attach a checkable forward commitment.
  • Match public repair to public defection, restoring the price of speaking up for witnesses as well as the injured party.
  • Rebuild a silent team's reporting culture by visibly rewarding the first mover who tests the new price of candor.
Simulations & scoring anchors

One-on-one: repairing the dismissed charge nurse — specific acknowledgment; costly apology; enforceable commitment.

Meeting: the safety huddle that learned silence — making challenge cheap; rewarding the first mover; installing mechanisms that outlive the meeting.

Debrief focus: a real repair, specified — or a first mover's loop the learner will close visibly.

Round 4 · Payer relations · C3

The Contract Table — Negotiation and Bargaining

BATNA · interests vs. positions · anchoring · the negotiator's dilemma
Learning objectives
  • Compute and price a BATNA before negotiating, and identify actions that improve it away from the table.
  • Excavate interests beneath stated positions with questions that invite information rather than defense.
  • Neutralize an anchor by re-anchoring on objective criteria, then pivot to integrative ground.
  • Construct value-creating trades across asymmetric interests — then claim a defensible share with a rationale the counterpart can ratify internally.
  • Forge a unified internal mandate: a true reservation point, ordered red lines, bounded authority, and an escalation protocol.
Simulations & scoring anchors

One-on-one: the payer renewal with hidden flexibility — interest discovery; value creation; relationship preservation.

Meeting: the pre-negotiation mandate huddle — forging the mandate; surfacing hidden constraints; the negotiator's charter.

Debrief focus: mapping a real upcoming negotiation — both parties' interests and BATNAs, one integrative option.

Round 5 · Rapid response · C1 C2

The Rapid Response — Coordination Games and Communication Under Pressure

Coordination vs. cooperation failures · focal points · common knowledge
Learning objectives
  • Differentiate coordination failures from cooperation failures, and select convergence fixes versus payoff fixes accordingly.
  • Explain how protocols and standard concentrations manufacture focal points, and how closed-loop communication creates common knowledge.
  • Escalate a clinical concern through graded assertiveness — concern with data → CUS → two-challenge rule — without skipping rungs or folding.
  • Lead an emergency response with named role assignments, demanded confirmations, and licensed call-outs; close every loop audibly.
  • Conduct a two-minute team debrief that converts one event's coordination into the team's standing practice.
Simulations & scoring anchors

One-on-one: 03:10 graded assertiveness with an anchored senior physician — assertion ladder; data-anchored advocacy; closed loops.

Meeting: leading the rapid response — closed-loop completion rate; surfacing held information; the team debrief.

Debrief focus: where the learner's real team runs on assumed rather than common knowledge.

Round 6 · Ethics committee · C5 C6

The Allocation Meeting — Fairness, Ethics, and the Ultimatum at the Bedside

Ultimatum game · procedural vs. distributive justice · crisis standards
Learning objectives
  • Explain why fairness perceptions function as real payoffs, and predict how excluded stakeholders reject illegitimate processes.
  • Distinguish procedural from distributive justice, and defend pre-committed transparent criteria over ad hoc bedside discretion.
  • Apply utilitarian, deontological, and justice frameworks as complementary error-detectors, articulating what each protects that the others miss.
  • Deliver a devastating decision without armor: the plain answer first, emotion named and legitimized, process transparent, promises scoped to actual authority.
  • Facilitate sincere framework conflict toward a defensible, revisable process — with the missing voice given real authority, not speaking time.
Simulations & scoring anchors

One-on-one: the family room — honesty without armor; empathy markers; process transparency.

Meeting: the ethics committee protocol review — framework stewardship; voice for the missing; a defensible process produced.

Debrief focus: the learner's default framework under pressure — and what the others would protect.

Round 7 · Innovation office · C3 C4 C5

The Coalition — Politics, Alliances, and Getting Innovation Adopted

Pivotal players · sequencing · the ethics of influence
Learning objectives
  • Map stakeholders by pivotality (whose movement changes the outcome) rather than seniority, using the Shapley intuition.
  • Sequence recruitment so pivotal skeptics get design access while the proposal can still move — objections requested before support.
  • Surface operative objections beneath stated ones, and convert them into specifications the skeptic co-authors.
  • Offer skeptics real instruments — kill clauses, overrides, audit triggers — and explain why instrumented opposition protects adoption.
  • Tailor one true proposal to different stakeholders' interest-languages while passing the exposure test: all versions cohere if compared.
Simulations & scoring anchors

One-on-one: recruiting the pivotal skeptical chief — surfacing true interests; design access granted; transparent influence.

Meeting: the governance vote — tailoring without forking; moving the genuine undecided; converting oppositions into governance roles.

Debrief focus: a real initiative mapped as a coalition problem; the honest pitch for each party.

Round 8 · Strategy retreat · Capstone · all competencies

The System Redesign — Mechanism Design and Leading at Scale

Incentive compatibility · Goodhart's law · the pre-mortem discipline
Learning objectives
  • Diagnose organizational dysfunctions as equilibria of badly designed mechanisms — asking what the rules pay for before who failed.
  • Redesign measures and incentives to the standard: gaming the metric and doing the right thing become the same behavior.
  • Run the pre-mortem ("how would I hit this number without doing the work?") and attach counter-metrics to every target.
  • Translate a mechanism redesign into financial language — downside, discovery cost, exit — while defending its load-bearing terms against gutting simplifications.
  • Design humane transitions for those the old rules rewarded, and lead a multi-stakeholder redesign end-to-end using all prior modules' skills.
  • Produce a personal leadership playbook: default tendency, counter-practice, one influenceable mechanism, one standing ritual.
Simulations & scoring anchors

One-on-one: penciling the redesign with the CFO — financial translation; mechanism integrity; owning the history.

Meeting (capstone): the strategy retreat — mechanism diagnosis without villains; running the room; trade-offs priced; a mechanism shipped with owners, counter-metrics, and a sunset.

Debrief focus: the summative playbook across all eight rounds.
